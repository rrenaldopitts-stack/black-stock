Quest 01 / About 5 minutes / Ownership badge

Think like an owner.

A stock represents ownership in a company. A share is one unit of that stock. Buying a share makes you a small part-owner, not an employee and not someone the company promises to pay back.

The stock market brings buyers and sellers together. Most everyday trades exchange existing shares between investors. A company can raise money by issuing new shares, such as through an initial public offering (IPO).

A quick example: your slice of the business

Imagine a fictional company with 1,000 equal shares. Owning 10 means owning 1% of those shares. If the price moves from $10 to $8, your 10 shares are worth $80 instead of $100. Ownership does not protect you from losses.

Culture connection: a music royalty is not a stock share

Black artists and entrepreneurs bring powerful ownership questions into music, film, fashion, and technology. Those questions can help you learn to ask: what asset would I actually own?

A music royalty is a payment tied to specific rights and agreements. A stock share represents equity in a company. Buying shares in a public entertainment company does not give you ownership of an artist's masters or a direct right to their royalties. Read what an investment actually grants, not just the story around it.

From the block to the market: supporting a business versus owning shares

Buying a book from a Black-owned bookstore supports its sales, but does not make you a shareholder. Many local businesses are privately held, and their ownership interests are not available on a public stock exchange. Supporting a business, starting one, and buying public shares are different paths with different responsibilities and risks.

Investors may earn money if they sell shares for more than they paid, or if a company pays dividends. Neither is guaranteed. A stock can lose some or all of its value, and dividends can be reduced or stopped.

Quest 02 / About 5 minutes / Risk-aware badge

Play the long game.

Prices rise and fall. A one-day chart cannot tell you whether an investment fits your life. Your goals, how long you can leave money invested, and your ability to absorb losses matter more than a trending ticker.

Diversification means spreading investments across different holdings, industries, or asset types. It can reduce the impact of one investment doing poorly. It cannot eliminate market risk or guarantee a profit.

What is an ETF?

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) holds a basket of assets and trades on an exchange. A broad-market ETF may hold many companies, while a sector ETF may focus on one industry. Not every ETF is diversified: some are concentrated, leveraged, or designed for short-term strategies. Review the holdings, strategy, risks, and fees.

What is compounding?

Compounding is growth that can build on earlier growth when earnings remain invested. For illustration only, $100 growing at a hypothetical 5% annually becomes $105 after one year and $110.25 after two, before fees and taxes. Actual returns vary and can be negative. This example is not a forecast.

Fees matter. Fund expense ratios, account fees, and other costs can reduce returns. Compare the full costs, not just a commission-free label.

Community connection: shared values still need independent research

A business can reflect your identity or values and still be a risky investment. Representation matters, but it does not guarantee returns. Read the financial information and risks, and do not let an online group or someone you trust pressure you into an investment. Supporting Black enterprise and building a diversified portfolio are not the same decision.

Quest 03 / About 5 minutes / Game-plan badge

Make your first game plan.

Building a legacy does not require rushing into a trade. A thoughtful first step may be learning more, building emergency savings, or addressing high-interest debt before investing.

  1. Name your goal. What is the money for, and when might you need it? Money for near-term essentials may not belong in volatile stocks.
  2. Check your foundation. Consider your essential expenses, emergency buffer, debt, and whether you can afford investment losses.
  3. Understand your account. Learn the differences between taxable brokerage and retirement accounts, including eligibility, taxes, fees, and withdrawal rules.
  4. Research before committing. Read disclosures and check a professional or firm's registration. Avoid anyone promising guaranteed high returns.
Can I start with a small amount?

Some brokerages offer fractional shares, letting you buy part of a share. Minimums, available investments, fees, and transfer rules vary. Starting small does not remove risk, and there is no obligation to invest before you are ready.

Black wealth-building has a history that includes both community resilience and unequal access to financial opportunity. Better information is one tool, not a cure for structural barriers. Your pace and circumstances deserve respect.

Your legacy, your definition

Imagine what you want money to make possible: a secure retirement, space to create, a home, education, or support for family or chosen family. You do not have to carry everyone's future on your own. Choose a goal that respects both your values and your current needs.
